https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13702

**Inverse scaling:** larger, more capable models produce less faithful CoT on most tasks. The 13B model relies more on its reasoning than the 175B model on 7/8 tasks. The authors attribute this to larger models already being confident enough to answer without the CoT, making the generated reasoning more post-hoc.

In short, CoT can be relatively faithful under the right conditions (certain tasks + not-too-large models), but on many settings—especially with stronger models—it is often post-hoc explanation rather than the actual causal reasoning path.

## 1. How would you actually run an experiment related to this work? (concrete setup, models, data, procedure, baselines)

**Minimal replication / extension setup:**

- **Models:** Open models of varying sizes (e.g., Llama-3/3.1 or Qwen2.5 family: 8B, 14B/32B, 70B). Prefer instruction-tuned or RLHF-style models if available; also run a base pretrained version for comparison.
- **Data:** Same or similar multiple-choice benchmarks (AQuA, LogiQA, ARC-Easy/Challenge, MMLU subsets, OpenBookQA) + a synthetic controlled task (e.g., multi-digit addition or simple logic puzzles of graded difficulty). Use 100–500 questions per task. Sample 10–50 CoT trajectories per question (nucleus sampling, temperature 0.7–0.8).

**Procedure:**

- Generate full CoT + final answer for each question.
- **Early answering:** Truncate after each sentence (or token chunk); force the model to answer from the partial CoT; measure agreement with the original final answer.
- **Adding mistakes:** Use a smaller model or a prompt to insert a plausible error into one intermediate step; regenerate the remainder; check whether the final answer changes.
- **Controls:** paraphrasing experiment and filler-token (“...”) baseline of matched length.
- Compute AOC-style metrics (or simpler % answer-change rates) and accuracy with vs. without CoT.

**Baselines:** No-CoT direct answering; CoT with temperature 0; human-written CoT (if available); and a “process supervision” or decomposition prompt as a positive control for higher faithfulness.

## 2. What would you measure? (specific metrics, success criteria)

**Primary faithfulness metrics:**

- % of cases where truncating or corrupting the CoT changes the final answer (higher = more faithful).
- AOC (or area-under-curve of answer-stability vs. truncation point).
- Correlation between early-answering and adding-mistakes results.

**Performance metrics:** Accuracy / expected calibration error with full CoT vs. no CoT vs. corrupted CoT.

**Success criteria for “more faithful”:** Statistically significant increase in answer-change rate under interventions while preserving or improving accuracy; consistency across the early-answering and mistake-injection tests.

**Secondary:** How often the model explicitly acknowledges a contradiction when a mistake is inserted; length and step count of CoTs.

## 3. What if X goes wrong? (failure modes, confounds, alternative explanations)

- **Sampling variance:** CoT is stochastic; low sample counts can make AOC noisy. Mitigate with more samples or temperature sweeps.
- **Intervention quality:** Injected “mistakes” may be unnatural or out-of-distribution, so the model’s reaction may not reflect normal behavior. Use a separate model or human validation for mistake generation.
- **Prompt sensitivity:** Results depend heavily on the exact CoT prompt and answer-extraction format. Always report multiple prompts.
- **Confound: model already knows the answer:** On easy or memorized items the CoT is irrelevant by construction. Filter for questions the model gets wrong without CoT, or use novel synthetic tasks.
- **Alternative explanation:** The model may be using the CoT as a “scratchpad” for computation whose intermediate states are not verbalized in the way we expect, or the final answer is determined early in the forward pass and the CoT is just role-play. Interventions on the generated text cannot fully rule this out without mechanistic tools (e.g., activation patching).
- **Evaluation leakage:** If you use the same model family to judge faithfulness, circularity is possible.

## 4. How does this relate to existing work? (position relative to other papers or directions)

This paper is one of the foundational empirical studies of CoT faithfulness (as opposed to just performance). It sits alongside:

- Turpin et al. (2023) “Language Models Don’t Always Say What They Think,” which showed models often fail to mention biasing features that affect their answers.
- Later work on process supervision, debate, and decomposition (e.g., Radhakrishnan et al. on question decomposition improving faithfulness).
- Inverse-scaling literature (McKenzie et al.).
- Mechanistic interpretability efforts that try to go beyond behavioral interventions (activation patching, circuit analysis of CoT).

It is primarily a measurement paper that provides practical intervention-based tests rather than a method for improving faithfulness.

## 5. What is a minimal version of this that you could run with open models and limited compute?

Use Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct or Qwen2.5-7B/14B on a single GPU:

- 100 questions from AQuA + 100 from ARC-Easy.
- Generate 10 CoT samples per question.
- Run early-answering (truncate after 1, 2, and 3 sentences) and a simple mistake-injection prompt.
- Compute % answer-change rate and accuracy delta.

Total cost: a few hours on a consumer GPU or a modest cloud instance. This is enough to see the task-dependence and roughly check the size trend if you also run a smaller (1–3B) model.

## 6. What is the key open question or limitation this paper leaves for follow-up research in AI safety?

The central open problem is: how do we make CoT (or other verbalized reasoning) reliably faithful as models become more capable, especially under RLHF / outcome-based training that only rewards final-answer correctness?

The paper shows that faithfulness is fragile and tends to degrade with scale. Key follow-ups include:

- Training methods (process supervision, faithfulness-aware RL, constrained decoding, or decomposition) that increase causal dependence on the stated reasoning.
- Better detection of unfaithful CoT in the wild (without requiring interventions on every sample).
- Understanding the mechanistic conditions under which models ignore their own CoT, and whether this generalizes to more advanced reasoning techniques (tree-of-thought, tool use, long-horizon agents).
- Whether faithfulness can be preserved or improved in frontier models, or whether post-hoc rationalization is an inherent side-effect of outcome-only training.
