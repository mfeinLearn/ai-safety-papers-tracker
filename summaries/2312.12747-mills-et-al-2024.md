# ALMANACS: A Simulatability Benchmark for Language Model Explainability

**Mills, Su, Russell, Emmons**  
[arXiv:2312.12747](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.12747)

## Summary

How should we measure whether an explanation method actually works? Most prior evaluations use bespoke tasks, making comparison difficult. ALMANACS introduces a standardized benchmark that scores explanations on **simulatability**: how much the explanations help an independent predictor better forecast the target model’s behavior on new inputs.

The benchmark consists of 12 safety-relevant topics (e.g., advanced AI risk, moral dilemmas, sycophancy, hiring decisions, power-seeking). Each topic uses templated scenarios with placeholders that create a train–test distributional shift, so the predictor must generalize rather than memorize. The evaluation is fully automated: another language model (GPT-4 in the paper) is given a small number of nearest-neighbor training examples (with or without explanations) and must predict the target model’s Yes/No probability on held-out scenarios.

The authors test several explanation methods—counterfactual examples, model-generated rationalizations, attention-based salience, and Integrated Gradients—against a no-explanation control. The result is sobering: **averaged across topics, no explanation method outperforms the explanation-free baseline**. Modest gains appear on individual topics (e.g., counterfactuals on sycophancy), but overall, current methods do not meaningfully improve the ability to simulate model behavior under distribution shift.

The incomplete note “using a simular took to act like” refers to this core design: using a simulator language model to act like (predict the behavior of) the target model, with and without the explanations.

## 1. How would you actually run an experiment related to this work? (concrete setup, models, data, procedure, baselines)

**Concrete setup**:

- **Target models**: Open models of interest (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B/32B, Mistral-7B). Generate Yes/No probabilities on the scenarios.
- **Data**: Use the released ALMANACS templates (or a reduced subset of 2–4 topics) from the authors’ GitHub. Keep the train–test placeholder shift.
- **Explanations to test**:
  - Model-generated rationalizations (prompt the target model to explain its answer).
  - Counterfactual nearest neighbors.
  - Simple salience (top attended tokens) or gradient-based attributions if feasible.
- **Simulator**: A stronger or different model (e.g., Llama-3.1-70B or an API model) that receives k nearest-neighbor examples and predicts the target model’s probability.
- **Procedure**:
  1. Collect target model probabilities on train and test scenarios.
  2. Generate explanations for training examples.
  3. For each test example, retrieve nearest neighbors, prompt the simulator with and without explanations, and record its predicted probability.
  4. Compare against the no-explanation control and simple baselines (predict average, pure nearest-neighbor).
- **Baselines**: No-explanation control, nearest-neighbor prediction, predict-the-mean, and (optionally) a linear probe on embeddings.

## 2. What would you measure? (specific metrics, success criteria)

- **Primary**: KL divergence (or normalized KL) between the simulator’s predicted probability and the target model’s true probability (lower is better).
- **Secondary**: Total variation distance and Spearman rank correlation.
- **Success criteria**: Statistically significant improvement over the no-explanation control on average (and ideally on a majority of topics), with the gain larger under stronger distributional shift.
- Also report per-topic results, since aggregate averages can hide topic-specific wins.

## 3. What if X goes wrong? (failure modes, confounds, alternative explanations)

- **Simulator is too strong**: The simulator may solve the scenarios itself rather than simulate the target model. Mitigate by using a weaker simulator or by making scenarios more idiosyncratic.
- **Nearest-neighbor retrieval fails**: Poor embeddings can make the retrieved examples unhelpful. Test multiple embedding models.
- **Explanations are noisy or misleading**: Rationalizations may contain plausible but unfaithful content that confuses the simulator (consistent with Turpin et al.).
- **Distributional shift is too weak or too strong**: If the shift is too small, even bad explanations work; if too large, nothing works. Ablate the degree of placeholder novelty.
- **Metric sensitivity**: KL can be unstable with extreme probabilities. Report multiple metrics and check calibration.
- **Alternative explanation**: The explanations contain useful information, but the simulator’s prompt format fails to leverage it. Try different prompting strategies.

## 4. How does this relate to existing work? (position relative to other papers or directions)

ALMANACS operationalizes the classic notion of **simulatability** (from Doshi-Velez & Kim and others) for language models and applies it to safety-relevant behaviors. It is complementary to the CoT faithfulness papers discussed earlier:

- Lanham et al. and Turpin et al. test whether CoT is causally used or mentions input biases.
- ALMANACS asks a more general question: do any common explanation methods (including rationalizations) help an external observer predict the model’s behavior under distribution shift?

It also connects to broader interpretability evaluation efforts and highlights a gap between methods that look promising in controlled settings and methods that actually improve prediction of model behavior.

## 5. What is a minimal version of this that you could run with open models and limited compute?

- Pick 1–2 topics (e.g., sycophancy and moral dilemmas) and ~200–500 scenarios total.
- Use a small open model as the target (Llama-3.1-8B or Qwen2.5-7B) and another small or medium model as the simulator.
- Compare only two conditions: no explanation vs. model-generated rationalization.
- Measure KL or absolute error on a held-out set.

This can be run in a few hours on a single GPU (or even CPU for very small models) and is usually sufficient to see whether rationalizations help or hurt relative to the no-explanation baseline.

## 6. What is the key open question or limitation this paper leaves for follow-up research in AI safety?

The central open problem is: **can we develop explanation methods that actually improve the ability of an external observer (human or model) to predict a language model’s behavior on new, safety-relevant inputs under distribution shift?**

Current methods largely fail this test. Important follow-ups include:

- Designing explanation techniques (or training objectives) explicitly optimized for simulatability.
- Testing whether more advanced methods (circuit analysis, activation patching summaries, process-supervised rationales) succeed where surface-level explanations fail.
- Understanding when and why explanations help vs. hurt (topic dependence, model size, type of shift).
- Bridging the gap between automated simulatability scores and human usefulness.

For AI safety, this matters because many oversight and monitoring strategies rely on the assumption that explanations make model behavior more predictable. ALMANACS suggests that assumption is currently poorly supported.
