# Chain-of-Thought Prompting (Wei et al., 2022)

**Paper:** [Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11903)  
**Authors:** Jason Wei, Xuezhi Wang, Dale Schuurmans, Maarten Bosma, Brian Ichter, Fei Xia, Ed Chi, Quoc Le, Denny Zhou (Google Research)

## Summary

Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting is a simple few-shot prompting technique that elicits multi-step reasoning in large language models by including intermediate natural-language reasoning steps in the demonstration examples.

Instead of the standard few-shot format of (question → answer), CoT uses triples of (question → chain of thought → answer). A chain of thought is a short sequence of natural-language sentences that break a problem into intermediate steps, similar to how a human might reason aloud (e.g., “Roger started with 5 balls. 2 cans of 3 tennis balls each is 6 tennis balls. 5 + 6 = 11. The answer is 11.”).

**Key findings:**

- **Emergent ability of scale.** CoT prompting provides little or no benefit (and can even hurt) for models below ~100B parameters. Smaller models produce fluent but logically flawed chains. Large gains appear only in sufficiently large models (LaMDA 137B, PaLM 540B, GPT-3 175B, etc.).
- **Strong empirical gains.** On arithmetic reasoning (especially GSM8K math word problems), PaLM 540B with just eight hand-written CoT exemplars achieved 58% accuracy, surpassing the previous state-of-the-art of fine-tuned GPT-3 175B + a verifier (~55%). Large improvements were also observed on other arithmetic datasets (SVAMP, ASDiv, MAWPS), symbolic reasoning (last-letter concatenation, coin flip), and some commonsense tasks.
- **Broad applicability.** The method works for arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning without task-specific fine-tuning. A single model checkpoint can handle many reasoning tasks via prompting.
- **Attractive properties.** CoT (1) allocates additional computation to harder multi-step problems, (2) provides an interpretable window into the model’s reasoning, (3) is applicable in principle to any task solvable via language, and (4) requires no model weight updates—only carefully chosen demonstrations.

The paper evaluates models from the GPT-3, LaMDA, and PaLM families (plus UL2 and Codex) using greedy decoding and a fixed set of manually written exemplars.

### 1. How would you actually run an experiment related to this work? (concrete setup, models, data, procedure, baselines)

- **Data:** GSM8K (math word problems), StrategyQA or CommonsenseQA (commonsense), and a symbolic task such as last-letter concatenation or a simple multi-step arithmetic/logic task.
- **Exemplars:** Manually write 4–8 high-quality chain-of-thought demonstrations for each task (or reuse the ones from the paper’s appendix when available). Keep the same exemplars across model sizes for fair comparison.
- **Procedure:**
  1. Standard few-shot baseline: prompt with (Q → A) pairs.
  2. CoT few-shot: prompt with (Q → CoT → A) triples.
  3. Optionally: zero-shot CoT (“Let’s think step by step”) as an additional condition.
  4. Use greedy decoding (temperature = 0) for primary results; optionally sample multiple generations and report majority vote (self-consistency).
  5. Evaluate on held-out test sets (e.g., GSM8K test, 100–500 examples for other tasks).
- **Baselines:** Standard prompting, zero-shot, and (if compute allows) a fine-tuned smaller model or a model with an external calculator for arithmetic.

### 2. What would you measure? (specific metrics, success criteria)

- **Primary metric:** Exact-match accuracy of the final answer (after extracting the answer from the generated text).
- **Secondary metrics:**
  - Intermediate step correctness (manual or automatic scoring of whether the reasoning steps are valid).
  - Faithfulness / consistency (does the final answer follow from the generated chain?).
  - Robustness to exemplar order and choice (multiple random seeds of exemplar order).
  - Scaling curve: performance vs. model size for standard vs. CoT prompting.
- **Success criteria:**
  - Statistically significant improvement of CoT over standard prompting on large models.
  - Evidence of emergence (little/no gain or negative gain on small models, clear positive gain on large models).
  - Qualitative inspection showing that successful CoTs contain coherent intermediate steps.

### 3. What if X goes wrong? (failure modes, confounds, alternative explanations)

**Possible failure modes and confounds:**

- **Illogical but fluent chains.** Small or medium models generate plausible-sounding but incorrect reasoning; accuracy can drop relative to standard prompting.
- **Unfaithful reasoning.** The model produces a CoT that does not actually determine the final answer (post-hoc rationalization). The paper notes that full interpretability remains open.
- **Exemplar sensitivity.** Performance depends heavily on the quality and style of the hand-written chains. Poor exemplars can hurt.
- **Length / computation confound.** Longer outputs simply give the model more tokens to “think,” independent of the content of the reasoning.
- **Data contamination.** Larger models may have seen GSM8K-style problems during pre-training; gains could partly reflect memorization rather than genuine reasoning.
- **Alternative explanations:**
  - CoT mainly acts as a better task specification / format cue rather than true multi-step computation.
  - Gains come from allocating more compute (more tokens) rather than from the logical structure of the chain.
  - The model is pattern-matching to the style of the exemplars rather than performing generalizable reasoning.

Mitigations: ablate chain length vs. content, test on out-of-distribution symbolic tasks, check for data contamination, and evaluate intermediate-step validity.

### 4. How does this relate to existing work? (position relative to other papers or directions)

- Builds directly on few-shot prompting (Brown et al., 2020 / GPT-3) and shows that the same paradigm can elicit reasoning when intermediate steps are included.
- Contrasts with earlier rationale-augmented training or fine-tuning approaches (e.g., Ling et al., 2017; Cobbe et al., 2021 GSM8K verifier) that require large amounts of labeled rationales and model updates.
- Related to neuro-symbolic methods that use formal languages, but CoT stays entirely in natural language.
- Precursor to later techniques such as self-consistency (Wang et al., 2022), zero-shot CoT (“Let’s think step by step”), Tree-of-Thought, and many reasoning-focused post-training methods.
- Positioned as an emergent ability of scale, aligning with the broader “emergent abilities” literature (Wei et al., 2022).

### 6. What is the key open question or limitation this paper leaves for follow-up research in AI safety?

**Key open question for AI safety:**

> To what extent are the intermediate reasoning steps *faithful* to the model’s actual computation, and can we trust chain-of-thought as a reliable window into (or control mechanism for) model behavior?

The paper highlights interpretability as a benefit but explicitly notes that “fully characterizing a model’s computations that support an answer remains an open question.” Later work has shown that models can produce coherent-looking CoTs that do not causally determine the final answer (unfaithful reasoning / post-hoc rationalization).

**Safety-relevant limitations and follow-ups:**

- **Deceptive or unfaithful reasoning.** A model could generate a plausible safety-aligned chain of thought while internally computing a harmful or misaligned outcome. This undermines the use of CoT for oversight, auditing, or process-based supervision.
- **Scalability of oversight.** If CoT is only reliable at very large scales and only for certain tasks, it may not be a robust tool for monitoring frontier models.
- **Reward hacking / Goodharting.** Optimizing for “good-looking” chains (via RL or process supervision) can produce chains that look correct but are not causally linked to the answer.
- **Open research directions:** Develop methods to verify faithfulness of CoT (e.g., causal interventions, consistency checks, mechanistic interpretability of the chain), study whether CoT can be used for scalable oversight or as a target for process supervision, and examine whether models can be trained or prompted to produce reliably faithful reasoning.

These questions remain central to using chain-of-thought (and its descendants) for alignment and oversight.
